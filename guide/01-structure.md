# 1. Structure and pacing

A faceless video has no presenter to carry it. Nothing on screen asks the viewer to stay; only the shape of the video does. This chapter is about that shape: how a video earns its first ten seconds, how it keeps moving, and how it ends so that the viewer remembers it.

## The unit of a faceless video is the scene

A faceless video is a sequence of scenes. Each scene is one spoken thought, one picture, and a few seconds of the viewer's attention. Everything else in this playbook (the voice, the captions, the footage, the cut) is built on that unit, so it is worth being strict about what a scene is:

- **One idea per scene.** The narration of a scene makes one point, and the picture illustrates that point. A scene that makes two points will get a picture for one of them and leave the other naked. If you catch yourself writing "and also" in a scene, it is two scenes.
- **A scene is as long as the thought.** The cut follows the narration, not a stopwatch. A scene that needs one sentence gets one sentence; a scene that needs three gets three. What you avoid is the scene that runs on: once a picture has been on screen for the length of a long breath, the eye starts looking for something new, and a long scene with a static picture is where viewers leave.
- **A scene has a title that nobody hears.** The title is for you and for the picture search: a concrete noun phrase that says what should be on screen ("Three Hearts, One Team", "Blue Blood, Built on Copper"). "Introduction" and "Conclusion" are not titles; they tell the picture search nothing.

Go Motion plans and edits in exactly this unit. The script you review is a list of scenes, each with a title and a narration, and production attaches a voice, timed captions and a picture to each of them. When you edit, you edit a scene; when you swap a picture, you swap it for a scene.

## The three jobs of a short video

Most faceless videos under a few minutes have the same skeleton, whatever the topic: a hook, a body, and a payoff. The skeleton is boring; the craft is in making each part do its job.

**The hook says what the viewer gets.** The first scene is not an introduction and not a welcome. It is a promise: in the next minute you will understand X, or here is the thing that makes Y surprising. The example in this repository opens with "In this video, you will learn why octopuses have three hearts, why their blood is blue, and how this anatomy helps them survive in cold, deep water." Three concrete promises, and the video then keeps each of them. A hook that promises nothing ("Today we're going to talk about octopuses") is skipped.

**The body alternates claim and proof.** Each body scene states one thing and then makes it real: a number, an example, a comparison, a mechanism. "When an octopus swims, its systemic heart stops beating" is the claim; "swimming exhausts the animal, because less oxygen reaches its body, which is one reason octopuses usually crawl along the seafloor" is the proof. A body that is all claims sounds like a lecture; a body that is all examples never gets to the point.

**The payoff lands the promise.** The last scene answers the question the hook asked, or shows the consequence of everything before it. In the example, the payoff is the one sentence the brief asked for: two hearts feed the gills, one feeds the body, and copper-based blood carries oxygen in the cold deep; the viewer leaves with one design instead of three facts. Do not end with "thanks for watching" or "let me know in the comments"; end on the idea, and let the platform's end screen do the rest.

## Ten shapes of a script

The skeleton above bends into different shapes depending on what the video is for. Go Motion ships ten writing styles, each named after the hook it opens with, and they are a useful vocabulary even if you write by hand: the opening decides how the rest of the video has to move and what its ending owes the viewer.

| Shape | Opens with | Moves by | Ends with | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Curiosity gap / Open loop** | something the viewer wants to know and is not told yet | the answer released in parts, each scene leaving one small question open | the full answer, then one thing the viewer can do with it | reveals, trivia, tutorials, history, documentaries |
| **Bold claim / Counter-intuitive** | a common belief or habit, flatly rejected | the claim, why the usual way fails, the better way; evidence at every step | what changes when you do it the better way | money, productivity, health, skills, tool reviews |
| **Direct question** | one specific question about a problem the viewer has | the answer straight away, then each scene answering or advancing one question, spoken to "you" | no question left open | problem-led topics: knowledge, money, productivity, relationships |
| **List / Number promise** | how many points are coming and what they will get the viewer | the points in order, each number said aloud, each with its own example; no overlaps, and the count the hook gave is the count delivered | what the list is worth as a whole | tool picks, tips, pitfalls, rankings |
| **Striking number / Result first** | the result itself, in its most concrete form | the steps behind it, one per scene, in exact terms | a method the viewer could repeat | case studies, results, data-driven stories |
| **Call out the mistake / Stop doing X** | a habit that is costing the viewer the result they want | why it fails and what works instead, the wrong way set against the right way; direct, never mocking | the right way and what it gets them | tutorials, skill fixes, tool how-tos |
| **Start mid-story / Story immersion** | the tensest moment: a time, a place, an action, no background | back to what led there, then on to what followed, in short sentences that paint the scene | how it turned out | stories, history, profiles, documentaries |
| **Audience callout / Direct address** | one group or one situation, named so precisely that the right viewers know it is for them | a fix for that group's actual problem, spoken to "you" | the next step for that viewer's situation | niche audiences, stage-specific problems, solo businesses, professions |
| **Myth vs reality / Before and after** | a common belief set against what is actually true | the myth, the truth, the evidence, what to do, one step per scene | what to do instead | money, health, growth, any field full of misinformation |
| **Fear warning / Loss aversion** | a loss the viewer is already taking without noticing | the loss made concrete, with a number that can be checked or a real situation, then what to do about it | something the viewer can do about it today | money, health risks, time management, pitfalls |

Several of these shapes end on an action. That is still ending on the idea: the action comes out of the content, something the viewer can try, check or stop doing, never a request to like or subscribe and never a thank-you for watching.

Each of these hooks is a promise, and the warnings that come with them are all about keeping it:

- **A curiosity gap needs a real payoff.** A question the video cannot actually answer, or answers with "it's complicated", is bait. What the shape holds back is the full answer, not the interest: every scene before the end still gives the viewer something new.
- **Numbers and evidence go in only when they can be checked.** The bold claim, the striking number, myth versus reality and the fear warning all stand on evidence, and it comes from your own material or from a source the scene carries, as [chapter 2](02-writing-for-the-voice.md#facts) describes. Where no reliable number exists, a concrete description of what happened does the same job without rounding a guess into a statistic. And one case is an example, not a rule.
- **A story shape never licenses fiction about real people.** Opening in the middle of the action still means the time, the place and the words are the ones that happened.
- **A fear warning must not exaggerate.** It names a real loss at its real size. A warning that inflates the risk to hold attention is a scare, and the channel's next warning will not be believed.

## Pacing

Pacing is the rhythm of scene lengths and of what each scene asks from the viewer.

- **Vary the length.** A run of scenes that are all the same length feels mechanical. Let a mechanism scene breathe and cut a transition scene short.
- **Front-load.** The most surprising fact goes early, not into a "but here's the twist" at the end. Viewers decide in the first few seconds whether a video is for them; the twist at the end is only seen by the people who stayed.
- **One new thing per scene.** Density is the enemy of retention in a faceless video, because there is no face to help the viewer absorb it. If a scene introduces two new terms, split it or cut one.
- **Transitions are sentences, not words.** "Now let's look at…" is filler. The last sentence of a scene can hand over to the next ("That is the sky. Sunsets are a different story.") and the picture change does the rest.

## Long videos: outline first, then chapters

A ten-minute video is not a one-minute video written ten times longer. It has chapters, and the failure mode of long AI-written scripts is that every chapter reads like a separate video: each one re-introduces the topic, repeats facts from earlier chapters, and ends with its own little conclusion. The viewer feels the seams.

The approach that works, and the one Go Motion uses for longer scripts, is to plan before writing:

1. **Write an outline first.** The outline names the chapters, gives each one its facts and story beats, and makes sure no beat appears twice. It also plans one continuous line through the whole video, so the chapters are stages of one argument rather than a playlist.
2. **Give every chapter two hand-offs.** Each chapter knows how it picks up from the one before and how it hands over to the one after. A chapter in the middle must not conclude or summarise; it leads into the next one.
3. **Write chapters one at a time, continuing from the previous ending.** When a chapter is written, it gets the closing lines of the previous chapter verbatim and continues from them. That is what makes the seam disappear: the first sentence of chapter three follows the last sentence of chapter two, instead of starting over.
4. **Only the last chapter ends the video.** The first chapter opens it; the last chapter closes it; everything in between keeps moving.

If you write long scripts by hand, the same discipline applies: outline, hand-offs, continue from the previous ending, and read the seams aloud.

## Worked example: "Why Octopuses Have Three Hearts" in five scenes

The example in `examples/why-octopuses-have-three-hearts/` was written from a one-paragraph brief that asked for one surprising fact per scene and a short takeaway. Its five scenes each do one job:

| Scene | Title | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A Blueprint for the Deep | the hook: three promises |
| 2 | Three Hearts, One Team | the setup: what each heart does |
| 3 | Why Swimming Is Exhausting | the surprise: the heart that stops |
| 4 | Blue Blood, Built on Copper | the second mechanism, and why it suits cold water |
| 5 | The Takeaway | the payoff: one design, one sentence |

Notice what is not there: no introduction scene before the promise, no scene that carries two facts, no "thanks for watching". The last scene is a recap, which is usually a mistake, and here it works because the brief asked for one and it compresses the whole video into a single sentence instead of repeating scenes 2 to 4. The video is just over a minute long because that is what five facts need.

## Checklist

- The first scene promises something concrete; the last scene delivers it.
- Every scene makes one point and has a title a picture researcher could work from.
- Each body scene has a claim and something that makes it real.
- No scene re-introduces the topic; no middle chapter concludes.
- The most surprising fact is in the first half.
- Read it aloud: every seam between scenes sounds like one voice continuing.
